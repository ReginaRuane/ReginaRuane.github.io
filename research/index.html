Research

Four strands, one problem: dependence that cannot be assumed away.

My research sits between statistics and computational social science — between network models and causal inference, and between the survey and the machine that now answers it.

The common thread is dependence that cannot be assumed away. When observations are connected, the network is not a nuisance parameter to be conditioned out; it is often the mechanism producing what we observe. That claim has a methodological half — what survives when a network is measured with error, or shifts underneath you — and an applied half, worked out in the legal infrastructure of eviction.

01

Eviction as a networked legal process

What produces a filing before anyone reaches a courtroom

Most statistical treatment of eviction models cases as independent draws conditioned on tenant attributes. My work argues the opposite: eviction is organized by repeat-player landlords, by the small set of attorneys who appear consistently, and by court scheduling. Working with 755,004 Philadelphia Municipal Court landlord–tenant records filed between 1969 and 2022, we find that ten plaintiff attorneys handled 82.2% of represented plaintiff-side cases per year on average, that large plaintiffs route 78.3% of their filings through a single attorney, and that nearly half of residential cases occur at addresses with a filing in the prior year. Downstream, courtroom outcomes turn on repeated attorney pairings and on reusable settlement templates rather than on case facts alone. The unit of analysis is the relationship, not the case.

02

Robustness and spillover in network models

Do conclusions survive when the network is measured with error?

A network model is always an approximation, and real networks are sparse, heterogeneous, and full of higher-order dependence that no single specification captures. This strand asks one question in two registers. Decision-theoretically, we let the posterior vary within a small Kullback–Leibler neighborhood and choose actions minimizing worst-case posterior risk, deriving sharp small-radius expansions for exchangeable graphs via graphon limits — including a universal critical exponent for how decision-level uncertainty explodes near percolation and fragmentation thresholds. Dynamically, network state-space models separate where interactions may occur, which the graph fixes, from how strong they are at each moment, which a latent state governs.

03

Data quality and classifier behavior

How training data propagates into what a classifier does

With Alan F. Karr, Director Emeritus of the National Institute of Statistical Sciences, I study the structure of classifier decision boundaries and how defects in training data propagate into classifier behavior — beginning with naive Bayes classifiers on short DNA reads and extending to large language models. Current work measures the variability of language-model output on survey data: if a model is going to stand in for a respondent, the first question is how much its answer moves when nothing that should matter has changed.

04

Models and computation for relational data

Building the machinery the three strands above depend on

Methodological work that the applied strands draw on: collapsed Bayesian block models that integrate out nuisance parameters so inference depends only on the partition; multiscale wavelet representations of the connectivity kernel that make resolution-indexed structure sparse and interpretable; copula-seeded sparse generators that separate degree heterogeneity from spatial locality and expose a degree-tail dichotomy; and generative models on Riemannian manifolds where the chart becomes a numerical preconditioner rather than a hidden modeling decision.
