Students & mentoring

Undergraduate research, scoped to a term.

Research at an undergraduate college can be immensely successful. I have seen it work at both Penn and Temple, and the benefit runs in both directions.

Students have contributed real components to the teams I have led — verifying that news articles were correctly labelled in a media bias detector, engineering platform capabilities for a geolocation data repository. One of my undergraduate mentees at Temple, Srikar Katta, wrote his first publication with me; he is now a doctoral student in computer science at Duke, publishing in top venues.

How I run it

Real question, real data

Students do better work when the question is not a teaching example. Every project above begins with data that exists in the world and a question nobody has answered.

A ten-week unit of work

Undergraduate research fails when the scope is a dissertation chapter. Each project is scoped so that a result exists at the end of one term, and so that the result can grow if the student stays.

Presentation is part of the work

End-of-year presentations are where students discover they know something. I build toward them from the first week.

Authorship where it is earned

Students who contribute get named. It is how Srikar Katta's first paper happened, and it changes what a student believes is possible.

Projects a student could start now

Each is scoped so that a result exists at the end of one ten-week term, and so that it can grow if the student stays.

One term

Housing court in a mid-size county

Needs: One statistics course, one programming course

Most counties publish a housing-court docket and an assessment roll. Linking them yields a filing-side dataset of exactly the kind behind the Philadelphia work, at a scale a student can hold in their head. The first question — who files, how often, and at which properties — is answerable in ten weeks and is genuinely unanswered for most jurisdictions.

One term

How much does a language model move?

Needs: One statistics course

Ask a language model the same survey question many times, varying only what should not matter. The result is a variance estimate, obtained cheaply, on a question that survey researchers are currently arguing about. Students learn replication, sampling, and variance decomposition on a problem where the answer is not in the back of the book.

One to two terms

Network descriptives for a real community

Needs: One programming course

Collect or obtain a small relational dataset — a club, a department, a citation neighborhood — and work through degree distributions, clustering, assortativity, and what a block model does and does not recover. The pedagogical value is in discovering how much a single observed graph fails to determine.
